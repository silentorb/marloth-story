Yes — I think **“the home is a hub” is an even better way of distinguishing** ***Pride and Prejudice*** **from** ***Mansfield Park*** than raw character count.

The important thing is not simply *how many people live at Longbourn*. It's that **Longbourn is an active node in the social network**. People leave it, arrive at it, and pass through it. The Bennet household is simultaneously a family home, a place where outsiders are received, and a staging point for movement through the surrounding social world.

### Longbourn has unusually high "hubness"

You can almost map *Pride and Prejudice* as a series of excursions from and returns to Longbourn:

**LONGBOURN**

* ↓ Bennet girls go walking/visiting
* ↓ Jane goes to Netherfield
* ↓ Elizabeth goes to Netherfield
* ↓ Elizabeth visits Charlotte at Lucas Lodge
* ↓ Elizabeth travels to Kent
* ↓ visits Rosings
* ↓ travels to Pemberley
* ↓ Gardiners join her
* ↓ returns to Longbourn
* ↓ Lydia's movements disrupt the household
* ↓ Wickham/Lydia eventually return to the family orbit
* ↓ Darcy/Bingley eventually enter the Longbourn orbit

And crucially, **other people come to Longbourn**:

* Bingley and his sisters
* Mr. Collins
* Mr. Wickham
* various neighbors
* the Philipses
* the Lucases
* eventually Darcy and Bingley
* the wider Meryton social world

So Longbourn isn't just where the Bennets happen to live. **It's one of the places where the plot's social traffic gets processed.**

***

## Mansfield Park is almost the opposite

This is a subtle but important distinction.

Mansfield Park has a **larger household**, but it doesn't have quite the same *porousness*.

The house feels more like an **institutional center of gravity** than a railway station.

People are there because they're members of the family, dependents, guests staying for an extended period, or people with a specific relationship to Sir Thomas's household.

And then when the narrative moves elsewhere, it often feels more like:

> **Now we are leaving Mansfield Park and entering another social world.**

Whereas in *P\&P* the movement often feels like:

> **Longbourn → social world → Longbourn → social world → Longbourn**

That's a significant difference in narrative architecture.

### Compare the two houses

| <br />                                           | **Longbourn**           | **Mansfield Park**                         |
| :----------------------------------------------- | :---------------------- | :----------------------------------------- |
| Resident population                              | Moderate                | **Large**                                  |
| Family members present                           | Many                    | **Many**                                   |
| Visitors                                         | **Very frequent**       | Frequent, but less continually circulating |
| Short visits                                     | **Extremely important** | Less structurally important                |
| People arriving unexpectedly / socially          | Common                  | Less characteristic                        |
| Characters repeatedly departing                  | **Very common**         | Common                                     |
| Characters returning home afterward              | **Very common**         | Less central                               |
| House as social meeting point                    | **Extremely strong**    | Strong                                     |
| House as narrative "base camp"                   | **Extremely strong**    | Moderate                                   |
| Sense of outside world flowing through the house | **Very strong**         | Comparatively weak                         |

And there's a lovely paradox here:

**Mansfield Park has more people in the house, but Longbourn feels more socially crowded.**

That's because *crowdedness* isn't equivalent to occupancy.

***

### Longbourn is a "hub"; Mansfield is a "world"

I'd actually give the two houses different spatial metaphors.

**Longbourn = hub**

The house is embedded in a dense network of nearby houses and people. Characters circulate through it.

**Mansfield Park = domain**

It's a large, internally organized household with its own hierarchy and inhabitants. The outside world comes into contact with it, but doesn't constantly flow through it.

That also explains something about the *feel* of the novels.

In *Mansfield Park*, when people go to Sotherton, Portsmouth, London, Bath, etc., **the departure itself has considerable narrative weight**. We're moving from one social environment to another.

In *Pride and Prejudice*, Elizabeth can spend the morning at Longbourn, walk to Meryton, have an encounter, visit someone, return home, receive someone else, and then go somewhere again. The geography feels **much more fluid**.

Meryton is practically an extension of Longbourn's social space.

***

## And this connects directly to your "party novel" observation

I think there are actually **three separate variables** behind the *P\&P* effect:

**1. Household density**\
How many people are already around?

**2. Hubness**\
How many social relationships pass through the household?

**3. Mobility**\
How frequently do characters leave the hub and return, bringing new relationships/information with them?

*P\&P* scores unusually high on **all three**.

*Mansfield Park* scores extremely high on #1, reasonably high on #2, but somewhat lower on #3.

That's why a Mansfield Park dinner can have **more people** than a typical Longbourn scene while the novel nevertheless feels less like a continuous party.

The *P\&P* characters are constantly **mixing the contents of different social containers**.

Jane comes back from Netherfield with news. Elizabeth comes back from Kent with revelations. Lydia comes back from Brighton married. Visitors arrive from other households. Mr. Collins brings Hunsford into Longbourn. The militia brings Wickham and a whole male social population into Meryton.

So **home is where the consequences of being elsewhere arrive**.

That's an unusually powerful narrative function for a domestic space.

And I'd go one step further: **Longbourn may be the most important "junction" in any Austen novel.** Not necessarily the largest household, not necessarily the most important estate, but the place where the greatest number of the novel's social trajectories repeatedly intersect.
